Top diagnosis: do not move budget from Google to Meta based on platform CPA. Meta is buying cheaper leads, but Google is producing materially better sales-qualified pipeline.

Evidence:
- Lead CPA: Google `$9,000 / 84 leads = $107`; Meta `$7,000 / 132 leads = $53`.
- SQL rate: Google `21 / 84 = 25%`; Meta `9 / 132 = 6.8%`.
- Opportunity rate: Google `8 / 84 = 9.5%`; Meta `2 / 132 = 1.5%`.
- Cost per SQL: Google `$9,000 / 21 = $429`; Meta `$7,000 / 9 = $778`.
- Cost per opportunity: Google `$9,000 / 8 = $1,125`; Meta `$7,000 / 2 = $3,500`.
- Pipeline per dollar: Google `$64,000 / $9,000 = 7.1x`; Meta `$16,000 / $7,000 = 2.3x`.

Meta’s reported CPA is especially suspect because it includes 7-day click and 1-day view attribution, and prospecting plus retargeting are blended. That can make Meta look efficient while hiding lower incremental quality.

Smallest safe next actions:
1. Keep Google Search budget stable, especially non-brand high-intent campaigns.
2. Split Meta prospecting and retargeting into separate reporting and budgets.
3. Re-optimize Meta toward CRM-qualified events, ideally SQL or opportunity, not platform leads.
4. Add stronger lead qualification on Meta: firmographic filters, required business email, company size, role, intent questions.
5. Run a limited Meta test, not a broad budget shift: move only a small amount from the weakest Google segment or incremental budget, and judge it on CRM outcomes.

Do not shift meaningful budget from Google to Meta yet. Do not compare platform CPA as the decision metric for a B2B SaaS sales-qualified opportunity goal.

After the change, measure:
- Cost per SQL
- Cost per opportunity
- Lead-to-SQL rate by channel
- SQL-to-opportunity rate by channel
- Pipeline generated per dollar spent
- Prospecting vs retargeting performance separately
- Incremental lift from Meta, excluding view-through-only credit where possible
